Diagnosis speaker notes · 75 seconds

“First, I’d check the wiring and connections. Assembly may introduce a loose terminal, wrong pin, damaged cable or missing return connection. An open circuit stops current; a resistive connection causes a voltage drop. With the battery isolated, I’d inspect the harness and verify continuity and polarity against the wiring information.

Second, I’d check the supply under starting load. The battery might look healthy at rest, but the motor draws substantial current at startup. Battery sag or resistance in longer cables and connectors can reduce the voltage available to the controller, triggering protection or limiting motor torque. I’d compare voltage at the battery and controller during a controlled start and check fault indicators.

Third, I’d investigate mechanical load. The installed motor now drives the gearbox, axle and wheels. Binding, misalignment or an engaged brake can require more starting torque than the system can deliver. I’d inspect the drivetrain while isolated, then compare a controlled uncoupled test if permitted. These checks distinguish a failed supply from a motor that is being asked to drive too much load.”

Why starting is different from running

A stationary DC motor has little or no back EMF: the voltage generated by rotation that opposes the supply. Starting current can therefore be high, unless the controller limits it. For a fixed-flux DC motor, torque is approximately proportional to armature current. Speed also depends on applied voltage, load and losses. “Current gives torque; voltage affects speed” is a useful starting rule, not a complete motor model.

A controller commonly uses PWM—rapid switching to vary average applied voltage—but the interview file does not specify Wallaby’s exact controller or PWM implementation. V = IR explains a voltage drop across a resistance; it does not by itself describe the entire motor.

Grounding assumption: floating traction supply.

Workers servicing the equipment, onboard operators and passengers may touch exposed metal. This candidate architecture keeps both DC poles insulated from the chassis, bonds exposed metal together, and uses insulation monitoring with a defined shutdown response. Confirm the real architecture and applicable requirements with MREx before implementation. If the traction supply is intentionally chassis-referenced, the fault-current path and automatic disconnection must instead be designed and verified for that arrangement. A fuse alone may not clear a first insulation fault on a floating system.

Design speaker notes · 95 seconds

“The required battery, motor controller and DC motor form the main power path. I’ve added DC-rated protection, isolation and a contactor, with dedicated return conductors, so normal motor current does not flow through the chassis.

For earth bonding, I’d connect accessible conductive parts, including the motor housing and equipment enclosures, to a common chassis bonding network. This helps control potential differences between metal parts. Bonding must work with insulation and a defined fault response. My proposed diagram assumes a floating DC traction supply, with insulation monitoring that requests shutdown on a detected insulation fault. I would confirm Wallaby’s actual grounding arrangement with the team; simply connecting the chassis to battery negative or to the track is not a justified design.

For EMC, the noise source is high-current switching in the traction circuit. It can couple through space or through shared wiring into the communication PCB and its cable. I’d keep power and return conductors close together, separate power and data routes, and use appropriate filtering and local decoupling. The communication branch gets a filtered supply, and I propose shielded twisted pair with a compatible interface. Shield termination is designed at enclosure entries for high-frequency performance. The cable interface and grounding details need confirmation. These measures address the noise source, coupling path and sensitive receiver together.”

Engineering details: avoid blanket shielding rules

At high frequencies, a low-impedance shield connection to the enclosure at cable entry is often important. A long thin shield pigtail can be inductive and ineffective. Bonding shields at both ends can help at high frequency where the chassis bonding architecture supports it, but low-frequency potential differences and circulating currents must also be assessed. The design depends on the cable, interface and vehicle grounding arrangement.

Twisted pair is most effective when the interface uses a compatible balanced or differential signalling method. The document says only that data is sent by cable; it does not identify CAN, RS-485 or another protocol. Ferrites, common-mode chokes, filters, transient suppression and isolated interfaces are options to select from measured noise and equipment requirements, rather than additions to make indiscriminately. If cables must cross, crossing approximately at right angles can reduce the length of close parallel coupling.

Before a powered test

Agree the procedure with the team: isolate for inspection, secure the locomotive, control wheel movement, use correctly rated measurement equipment and reconnect under supervision. Never measure resistance on an energised circuit. Work on exposed live circuitry belongs in an approved test procedure.

INTERVIEW DESK

Understand it. Then explain it.

Likely follow-up questions

Why can it work on the bench but fail in the locomotive?

Installation changes the wiring path, connections, supply conditions and mechanical load. A bench success demonstrates that the motor can work in that test setup; it does not prove the entire installed system is correct.

Why doesn’t a continuity test prove the wiring is good?

A meter uses a small test current. A connection may pass that test yet develop substantial voltage drop and heating at motor starting current. Visual inspection and a controlled voltage-drop test provide different evidence.

Why not assume the controller is broken?

It is possible, but test the simpler installation-related explanations first. Check that the controller receives valid supply, return and hardware enable/interlock signals and inspect its fault indicators. Working software does not prove the physical enable or power wiring is correct.

Does the earth bond make the chassis safe by itself?

No. Bonding connects exposed conductive parts to control potential differences. Protection also requires suitable insulation and a fault-detection/disconnection strategy for the actual grounding architecture. The first fault on a floating DC supply may not create enough current to blow a fuse.

Why do we need EMC if there is no radio?

The motor circuit can generate electromagnetic disturbances without intentionally transmitting radio data. Switching noise may enter the communication PCB through its supply or couple onto the data cable. EMC concerns their ability to function together.

What would you do if you did not know an answer?

State what you know, identify the missing specification, explain why it matters, and describe how you would verify it with the team. Do not invent Wallaby’s component ratings or claim the proposal has passed tests.
